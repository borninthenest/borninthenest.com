Why every mammal looks for a nest before giving birth

Your body already knows how to do this. Here's what helps it.

Home birth, explained by a mom who's done it. That's what these five free lessons are, right here on the site, no signup needed.

In the movies, birth is a woman screaming in pain while everyone rushes around her. Most of us have watched that scene a hundred times, and somewhere along the way we started believing it.

But what if birth doesn't have to be like that? Yes, there's intensity, and there can be pain. But pain and suffering aren't the same thing. Birth can be one of the most beautiful, powerful things you'll ever do. We've just been taught to see it through the wrong lens.

I'm Sybille, a mom of two. My first baby was born in a hospital with an epidural. I was completely numb and felt nothing. My second was born at home, in my own bed, with a midwife I trusted, and that birth showed me how calm and beautiful giving birth can be. Same body, completely different birth. The biggest difference was where I was and how calm and loved I felt.

As my midwife Sarah always said, pregnant women need to feel loved. That's what Born in the Nest is about.

Hospitals do beautiful, important work, and for some pregnancies they're exactly the right place. And for many of us, birth is also something our bodies were made to do.

So let's start with one question.

Close your eyes for a moment. Where would you feel calmest, most at ease, and most loved?

For many of us, the honest answer is somewhere familiar and quiet, with people we love. And that matters more than we're usually told.

Every mammal looks for a nest. A cat finds a quiet closet. A deer slips into tall grass. When an animal in labor feels watched or threatened, labor often pauses until she's somewhere safe again. Women have been giving birth for as long as there have been people, and we carry that same wisdom.

Here's the gentle science. Labor is guided by hormones, as researcher Dr. Sarah Buckley explains in a detailed review. Oxytocin, the "love hormone," powers contractions. Endorphins are your body's own comfort. When you feel safe, private, and undisturbed, these flow freely. When you feel afraid, rushed, or watched, stress hormones like adrenaline rise, and they can slow labor down. Midwife Ina May Gaskin calls this the Sphincter Law: the body opens best in privacy and calm, the same way none of us can relax in a bathroom with the door wide open. She even found that a relaxed jaw, soft breathing, and laughter help the body open.

Your heart and your womb are connected. Dr. Amali Lokugamage, an obstetrician who planned her own baby's birth at home, explores this in her book The Heart in the Womb. Oxytocin, the hormone that powers labor, is also the hormone of love, trust, and bonding. So when your heart feels safe and loved, your body can soften and open. When you're tense and scared, it tends to hold on. That's not weakness. It's your body protecting you and your baby.

So when you think about where to give birth, it's not only a medical question. It's also a question of where your body can relax enough to do what it was made to do.

A tiny practice for today: take three slow breaths, longer on the out-breath than the in-breath, and notice your shoulders drop. That softening is what we're making room for.
