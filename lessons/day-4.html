My midwives saw what I needed before I said a word

How midwives watch over you, and how to find comfort without an epidural.

My midwives felt a little like superheroes to me. They paid attention to everything: what I was eating, how I was sleeping, how I was really feeling, and the small things I'd never have thought to mention. Often they seemed to understand what I needed just by looking at me, before I said a word. That quiet attention was the most powerful thing I experienced, and it's a big part of how midwives care for you.

When I asked my midwives whether I needed a doula, they smiled and said, "We are your doula." They were with me the whole way, not coming and going on shifts.

Because the question every mom asks is "But what if something goes wrong?" And it's a good one. Feeling calm isn't only about candles and quiet. It's also about knowing someone skilled is watching, and that there's a plan.

How midwives keep watch

Your midwife listens to your baby's heartbeat regularly throughout labor, checks your blood pressure, pulse, and temperature, and watches how labor is progressing. She's trained to notice early signs that something needs more help, often long before it becomes urgent.

When a home birth might still be possible.

Ask before you rule it out. That's my biggest wish for you here.

In my first pregnancy, I was told at the hospital that I had high blood pressure. It turned out to be nerves: hospitals just made me nervous. That's simply my story, not medical advice, but it taught me to ask questions before deciding what was possible for me. With my second pregnancy, my midwife suggested I take my blood pressure at home every morning. I did, and it was completely normal. With my first baby, that high blood pressure was one of the reasons I was induced. So if blood pressure comes up for you, talk to your midwife about checking it at home, where you're relaxed.

So don't rule yourself out too fast. A breech baby or twins don't automatically take a home or birth-center birth off the table to talk about. Some experienced midwives can talk through your options with you, like trying to turn a breech baby before labor. I knew women at my birth center who had twins. Just talk to your midwife. Ask if she has experience with twins or breech babies.

Your due date is just a guess

My midwives could not care less about my due date. From the very beginning they told me the baby comes when the baby comes. So you have a birth month, but the exact day doesn't really matter. Hospitals often treat that date like a ticking clock. As you get close, they want you to come in, and if you go a day past it, everyone gets worried, and that can make you nervous too. With midwives, you just wait for your baby.

What a transfer usually looks like

In the rare case you need a transfer, your midwives know if something is off, and they have a plan to get you to the hospital. If plans change, your midwife comes with you and shares your notes. They've cared for many, many first-time moms, and the plan is the same for everyone.

Did you know? 🌿 No clock on the cord. You wait for your baby, not a schedule. Many home birth midwives wait to clamp the cord until it stops pulsing on its own. That's often a few minutes, sometimes much longer, with no clock to rush it. It's something to see. Wherever you give birth, you can ask about delayed cord clamping and add it to your birth plan.

Ways to find comfort

Remember Day 1? When you're scared, your body tenses up, and that makes labor harder. When you feel calm and cared for, your body can relax and do its work. Here's what helps:

  • Warm water. A birth pool or bath.
  • Movement. Swaying, walking, leaning over a ball or the bed.
  • Calm birth breathing. Slow breaths, long on the exhale, practiced for weeks before so your body knows them.
  • Touch and sound. Massage, counter-pressure on your lower back, low humming, a soft, loose jaw.
  • Laughter and love. Ina May noticed that laughter and affection help labor flow. They really are part of the toolkit!

None of this has to wait until the big day. Giving birth is a bit like running a marathon: you wouldn't do it out of the blue. You train and build your team ahead of time, and your body gets ready too. My midwives helped me practice breathing through each wave for nine months, so on the big day nothing felt unusual or off.

Today's practice: breathe in for 4, out for 6, and let your jaw go loose. Do it for two minutes tonight before sleep. A tip someone once gave me that I still use, even now that I'm not pregnant: practice a few slow breaths every time you stop at a red traffic light while driving.
