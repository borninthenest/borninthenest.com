Birth center vs hospital: what's the difference?

The short answer: A birth center is all about calm, and all about you and your baby. You are the most important person in the room. You know your team, and when it's go time, your team is there for you. A hospital is a big organization, so you'll have less control over who's there and how things go. You're more likely to feel like one of many patients, and a hospital also has to run as a business. Just keep that in mind, and notice how you feel when you visit.

By Sybille Mom of two · first birth in a hospital, second at home · Education only, not medical advice

My first baby was born in a hospital. I was completely numb and felt helpless.

What I wish I'd known is that I had choices. So let's look at two of them side by side.

A note on epidurals

When labor is induced, you're usually given Pitocin, a synthetic version of oxytocin. It pushes your body into labor before it's ready, and the contractions can feel very different: stronger and unpredictable, and much harder to manage. That's how it was for me before I got the epidural. And then I felt nothing at all, which was horrible. For me, it's not an option I would ever choose again. That's why so many moms who are induced end up needing an epidural. When labor starts on its own, your body prepares itself. The contractions build step by step, and you stay in control because your body knows what it's doing. Some moms also reach for an epidural because they're in a fear state and can't get it under control. Fear can slow labor down, or make you feel like you can't cope anymore. So a lot of it is mindset, and that's something you can prepare for.

Who cares for you

At a birth center, your midwives. That's what it's all about. Get to know them, spend time with them, and see if they're the right fit for you. The right midwife makes you feel calm, heard and loved, right from the first moment. A birth center is an inviting place you get to know really well, because you go there for your visits every month, so it feels a bit like a home away from home. It's beautiful and warm. Nobody rushes you, it's calm, and there are no harsh bright lights. And just like at home, you can invite whoever you want to your birth.

At a hospital, your care is usually led by an obstetrician, a family doctor or a midwife, with nurses by your side during labor. Staff work in shifts, so you'll likely meet completely different people, and it may not be your own doctor.

What's available

At a birth center, it's not about the epidural. It's about getting you ready, over nine months, for your big marathon. You get to know your team, and they get to know you. There's no epidural or pain medication there, because when you're prepared and calm, you don't need it. Instead you can labor in a warm birth tub, move freely, and cope by understanding your body and how its natural process works.

At a hospital, epidurals, doctors and an operating room are right there. Many hospitals also offer tubs, birth balls and dimmable lights, so ask what yours has.

How birth tends to go

Wherever you give birth, at home, in a birth center or in a hospital, you can have a good birth. The difference is how your body responds to the place. In a hospital, you're often in an unfamiliar room with people you've never met. For some moms that can make the body tense up and slow labor down, and when labor slows, interventions can become more likely. For others it doesn't change a thing. At home or in a birth center, you're with a team you know, in a place you've gotten to know, which can make it easier for your body to relax and do its work.

What it costs

I paid $5,600 to my birth center midwives. A hospital birth can cost a lot more or less depending on your plan. It depends on your insurance either way, so ask your insurer and each place for their prices. Ask the center for its price and what it includes, and ask the hospital what a birth there usually costs. Questions to help you choose

  • Where do I picture myself feeling calm and cared for?
  • Do I want an epidural available down the hall, or do I want to try other comfort tools first?
  • Who would care for me, and can I meet them before labor?
  • At a birth center: which hospital do you work with, and how would we get there if plans change?
  • At a hospital: can I move around, eat, use a tub and keep the lights low?
  • What does my insurance cover at each?
  • If you can't be yourself, can't ask any question you want, or feel pushed into certain things, that's a red flag, and you should pay attention to it.

Questions moms ask

Can I get an epidural at a birth center?

No, birth centers don't offer epidurals. Before you decide you'll want one, read our note on epidurals, so you know how they work and why so many moms end up needing one. Many moms find that with nine months of preparation and a team they know, they don't need one. And if you decide you want one during labor, you can always move to a hospital.

Do birth centers have doctors?

No, birth centers don't have doctors. They have midwives. For me, that's one of the best parts. Your midwives have been with many women through birth, and they're with you from the beginning to the end, so they know you and your baby inside and out. And it's not a ten-minute visit once a month. It's a much longer, deeper relationship.

Is a birth center cheaper than a hospital?

I paid $5,600 to my birth center midwives. A hospital birth can cost a lot more or less depending on your plan. It depends on your insurance either way, so ask your insurer and each place for their prices.

Can I have a midwife at a hospital?

Yes. Many hospitals have midwives. Ask your hospital or insurance company how to find one.

Can I make a hospital birth feel calmer?

Often, yes. Ask about dimming the lights, moving around, eating and drinking, using a tub, and who can be with you. Bring your own playlist and pillow. One important thing: write down everything that matters to you before labor starts, because once it begins you won't want to talk much. You'll have so much going on. Share it with your partner, and with your midwife if you have one, so they can speak up for you.
