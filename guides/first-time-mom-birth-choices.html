First-time mom? What no one tells you about your birth choices

The things I wish I'd known before my first baby.

My first baby was born in a hospital, the way most of us are told it should go. No one ever asked me the most important question: where do you want to give birth? I didn't even know it was a choice. No one told me I had other options until my second pregnancy. I want you to know sooner.

1. You get to choose where you give birth

In the US you can plan a birth at home with a midwife, in a birth center or in a hospital. ACOG, the professional organization of US obstetricians, says clearly that you have the right to choose, after learning the benefits and risks of each. Read the gentle overview of your birth options.

2. Midwives aren't only for home births

Certified nurse-midwives are licensed in all 50 states and DC, and they work in hospitals and birth centers as well as homes. Certified professional midwives specialize in birth at home and in birth centers, and they're licensed in 37 states and DC. Many families never meet a midwife simply because no one suggested it.

3. A birth center can be a beautiful choice for a first baby

For first-time moms with healthy pregnancies, the UK's NICE guideline explains that planning birth in a midwife-led unit, much like a US birth center, has the same outcomes for babies as a hospital, with fewer interventions. Planned home birth for a first baby comes with a small increase in serious problems for babies, about 4 more per 1,000 births. That's worth talking through with your midwife, and it's why I think of birth centers as the great second option.

4. First labors are often longer, and that's okay

First-time moms move from a home or birth center to the hospital more often than moms having another baby, usually because labor is long and slow rather than an emergency. In a US study of nearly 17,000 planned home births, first-time moms transferred about three times as often. Knowing this ahead of time helps you plan calmly instead of feeling like something went wrong.

5. You can ask questions, and you can switch

It's your pregnancy and your birth. You can interview midwives and doctors, ask about their approach, and change providers if something doesn't feel right. Questions I'd ask:

  • How many births have you attended, and what's your training?
  • Who is your backup, and who comes with you to births?
  • What's your transfer plan, and which hospital do you use?
  • How long are prenatal visits, and how often will we meet?
  • What does it cost, and will you help me check my insurance?

6. Your feelings matter to your body

Feeling safe, calm and loved isn't a luxury in labor. It helps the hormones of birth do their work. So the question of where to give birth isn't only a medical one. It's also a question of where your body can relax enough to do what it was made to do.
